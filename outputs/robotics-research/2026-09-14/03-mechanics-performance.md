# Brickgineers robotics: mechanics and Chromebook performance

Research date: 2026-09-14. Read-only source inspection at `/Users/ahmaadidrees/.codex/worktrees/brand-integration`, HEAD `8fd02590eae9f4073e1007e8dbe183f3ff7de138`. This report is the only file created by this research lane. No implementation, deployment, live-load test, or Chromebook benchmark was performed. Numerical budgets below are proposed acceptance targets, not measured capabilities.

## Recommendation

Keep Three.js/R3F/Rapier. Start with one student-built, two-motor rover on a flat test surface, with real left/right commands, measurable wheel spacing/radius, physically positioned sensors, obstacle avoidance, and a dependable reset. Compile the student's editable brick assembly into a separate simulation representation. Preserve the same motor/sensor interface when adding dynamic motion and articulated mechanisms.

The smallest useful first implementation is an explicitly **ideal-drive model**: differential-drive mathematics plus collision queries, with no promise of torque, tipping, traction, or climbing. Its collision envelope must be visible and honest. Do a bounded technical comparison against Rapier's raycast vehicle before committing to that model: if driving on the existing world's ramps, falling off edges, or pushing objects is essential to the first release, start with a dynamic chassis instead. Those requirements invalidate a permanently planar controller.

For later arms, grippers, drawbridges, and factories, introduce rigid subassemblies connected by a small number of motorized hinges/sliders. Do not make every ordinary brick its own dynamic body or fixed joint. An arbitrary decorative build and an arbitrary working mechanism are different promises; mechanical connectors, degrees of freedom, and supported assembly rules must be explicit.

The user's updated direction includes substantial SPIKE experience, grades 3+/possibly 4+, and futuristic turbo, flying and levitation pieces. The flat rover is a first verification slice, not a ceiling on that product. Add imaginative modules through the same attachment, command and sensor model; make their rules observable and consistent without presenting them as calibrated physical robotics.

## Three viable motion models

| Model | What it does well | Main costs and limits | Use here |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Ideal differential drive with a kinematic body and swept collision envelope | Predictable straight travel, turning, wheel circumference, sensor placement, loops, conditions, feedback control. Low dynamic-body/constraint cost. | Mass does not change acceleration; no genuine traction, tipping, suspension, wheel-ground forces, or physical pushing. Arbitrary rotation needs deliberate collision treatment. | First programming/geometry milestone on a flat course. |
| One dynamic chassis with raycast wheels, or a custom bounded ground-contact force controller | Gravity, chassis collisions, support on ramps, approximate suspension/traction, mass distribution effects if configured. Avoids a body/joint per wheel. | A wheel ray is not a solid wheel. Sharp steps, sidewall impacts, thin obstacles and bridging gaps can look wrong. Tire parameters are approximations; skid steering needs tuning and proof. | First choice to investigate when free-world terrain matters. |
| Dynamic wheel bodies connected by motorized revolute joints | Actual wheel contact geometry and reactions; reusable hinge model for arms and mechanisms. | More bodies, contact manifolds, constraints and solver sensitivity; wheel friction, axle frames, support geometry and mass ratios become product work. | Add when wheels climbing obstacles or joint-driven mechanisms are central lessons. |

Rapier's vehicle API exposes per-wheel connection points, suspension direction, axle, radius, engine force, braking, steering and contact information. `updateVehicle(dt, ...)` changes chassis velocity and must be called once for each physics step. This is a capable building block, not an automatically calibrated differential-drive robot. The installed binding also has an unusually named `setIndexForwardAxis` setter; use its actual signature. [Rapier vehicle API](https://rapier.rs/javascript3d/classes/DynamicRayCastVehicleController.html)

For the ideal model, with wheel radii `rL,rR`, angular speeds `wL,wR`, and track width `b`, derive `vL=rL*wL`, `vR=rR*wR`, `v=(vR+vL)/2`, and yaw rate `(vR-vL)/b`, using a documented sign convention. Changing actual wheel spacing must change turning; changing radius must change distance per rotation. A motor-power percentage is a command mapped to target speed, not a measurement of torque. The model assumes rolling without slip. WPILib's kinematics explicitly converts between chassis and wheel speeds using track width. [WPILib differential-drive kinematics](https://docs.wpilib.org/en/stable/docs/software/kinematics-and-odometry/differential-drive-kinematics.html)

**Rotation trap:** Rapier's built-in kinematic character controller only corrects translation. It does not sweep a turning long chassis. Setting its next quaternion can swing a student's overhang through a wall. For the smallest flat-course slice, use a conservative circular/cylindrical envelope containing the assembled robot and show its clearance outline. That is collision-safe for pure yaw but rejects some visually valid narrow passages. More accurate oriented compounds require bounded angular subdivision plus conservative sweep/overlap handling; a single end-pose test can miss a thin wall. Do not silently call that solved. [Rapier character-controller limitation](https://rapier.rs/docs/user_guides/javascript/character_controller/)

A dynamic body whose linear/angular velocity is overwritten every frame can still collide, but the controller continually fights external forces. Calling this realistic mechanics would be misleading. A force-based variant should apply bounded drive forces at support/contact locations and explicitly model lateral resistance; if that bespoke tire model grows complex, compare the built-in raycast vehicle or wheel hinges before continuing.

## Assembly compilation and editing lifecycle

Recommended data boundaries:

1. **Build document:** persistent brick IDs, dimensions, colors, local transforms, mechanical part definitions, explicit connector edges, motor/sensor ports, program, and spawn pose. No Rapier handles or transient world-space motion.
2. **Compiled assembly:** immutable, versioned output containing rigid groups, body-local collision shapes, mechanical joints, validated axes/anchors, mass properties, sensor mounts, drive configuration, and a mapping back to student-visible parts.
3. **Simulation instance:** disposable body/joint/controller handles, actual poses and velocities, program execution state, sensor samples, events, random seed, and elapsed simulation ticks.

Ordinary rigid attachments are grouped into one body per connected rigid component. A powered hinge separates two such groups; otherwise it cannot move them relative to each other. Multiple colliders may attach to one body. Rapier's official compound example demonstrates several offset shapes sharing a body. [Rapier compound example](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/dimforge/rapier.js/master/testbed3d/src/demos/compoundShapes.ts)

Do not infer a durable connection merely because bounding boxes touch. A visual editor's snapping/overlap rule is not yet a mechanical connection graph. Persist compatible connector pairs with local position, orientation/axis, ownership, and attachment type. A valid-looking motor housing needs an output axle connected to a wheel; a nearby wheel alone should not power the machine.

Before Run, validate finite dimensions and transforms, positive supported masses, nonzero wheel radius and track width, motor-to-wheel attachment, consistent axle alignment, drive-wheel support at spawn, missing/duplicate ports, illegal overlaps, world clearance, and the supported joint/body budget. For the initial ideal drive, require two parallel drive axes and compatible wheel placement. A wheel mounted vertically or above the floor needs an explanation, not a hidden correction. Ground-support checks cannot promise real stability until a dynamic model exists.

Build edits while running should request a normal transition: pause/stop, preserve the authored document, show the authored pose, apply the edit, recompile, then restart from spawn. Resizing invalidates geometry, collider shape, anchors, inertia and support checks; rebuild the affected compiled assembly after the committed drag, not every pointer move. During a drag, render a lightweight preview without mutating active physics bodies.

Allow speed/steering commands and bounded program parameters to change while running. Treat topology, dimensions, wheel positions, and joint changes as structural edits. A future live-edit mode can rebuild only an affected island with explicit velocity-transfer semantics, but should not be an initial requirement. Program editing can prepare a draft while the current version runs; execution changes must occur at a defined pause/restart boundary.

Reset destroys/recreates the simulation from the same compiled snapshot, clears contacts and command queues, resets the interpreter/sensor history and restores the seed. Teardown must remove controllers/joints/bodies and dispose owned allocations. A stale handle after remount must not send a command to a new body. An explicit future “keep final position” action would write a new build pose; ordinary simulation movement must never autosave over the build.

## Colliders, terrain, units and joints

Use authored primitive/convex collision shapes for moving assemblies. Merge adjacent compatible solid boxes where it preserves shape; keep holes and fork gaps open. A single convex hull would fill an arch or gripper cavity. Detailed stud/cavity render meshes should not automatically become dynamic triangle meshes. Rapier discourages dynamic triangle meshes and recommends convex decomposition; it also supports smaller-memory heightfields for terrain representable by a height grid. [Rapier collider guidance](https://rapier.rs/docs/user_guides/javascript/colliders/)

Keep studs visual for ordinary drivable surfaces in the first slice. Use a smooth support surface with the documented physical top height. Otherwise wheels hit each decorative stud and beginners observe contact jitter rather than useful cause and effect. Keep intentional ramps/steps physical and make collision previews available. A heightfield cannot represent a tunnel, bridge underside or overhang, so retain fixed convex/primitive sections for those geometries. Avoid tiny seams in a supposedly continuous road and test transitions between neighboring colliders.

Do not silently reuse avatar-tuned collision insets as robotics tolerances: the current part module has a 0.035 graphics-unit collider inset and stair shapes tuned to an explorer capsule. Reusing shape-generation structure is sensible; wheel clearance, steps and narrow gripper gaps require separate checks.

Rapier recommends meters, seconds and kilograms with a graphics-to-physics scale. Keep the student's dimensions in studs/plates and use one versioned conversion module. A possible **design choice, not an established physical measurement**, is 1 stud = 0.05 simulation meters; existing 0.62 graphics units/stud then implies 12.4 graphics units/meter. Preserve all axes uniformly, including the current plate/stud height ratio. Start with a rover-scale object rather than literal millimeter-sized toys, and do not claim real brick dimensions or motor specifications. `lengthUnit` adjusts internal tolerances; it does not convert authored lengths or automatically rescale gravity. [Rapier units guidance](https://rapier.rs/docs/user_guides/javascript/common_mistakes/), [World lengthUnit API](https://rapier.rs/javascript3d/classes/World.html#lengthUnit)

If teaching mass distribution later, give materials/parts explicit estimated mass and inertia, or a documented density model. Avoid both adding complete body mass and allowing dense colliders to add it again. Overlapping simplified colliders can double-count volume; explicit aggregate mass properties are safer when collision geometry is aggressively simplified. Sensor colliders should not accidentally add sensor-volume mass.

Use revolute velocity motors for wheels and position motors plus limits for a hinge/arm; use a prismatic joint for a slider. Rapier motors implement PD-style position/velocity control. Tune a small stable assembly before expanding catalogs. Neighboring connected bodies may need contacts disabled locally, but do not disable all robot self-collisions if that lets a gripper pass through the robot. [Rapier joint motors](https://rapier.rs/docs/user_guides/javascript/joints/)

**Verified API gap:** installed `dynamics/impulse_joint.d.ts:130–155` exposes `setLimits`, `configureMotorModel`, and position/velocity motor configuration, but no public motor maximum-force/impulse setter. No matching setter was found in the installed declaration set. The guide mentions maximum impulse more generally; do not infer a usable 0.19.2 JavaScript torque-limit API from that sentence. If torque/stall is a lesson, verify a suitable binding or implement a separately tested bounded equal-and-opposite torque controller around an unpowered hinge. Do not run that controller and a velocity motor simultaneously. Supply/gear efficiency, backlash, motor heating, deformation and brick clutch strength are additional models, not effects Rapier will supply automatically.

For arms, begin with one fixed base and one moving rigid group. For a gripper, distinguish actual frictional grasping from an assisted attach/release rule; an attachment shortcut cannot teach grip-force sufficiency. Drawbridges are a natural single-hinge next step. Factories can begin with a few hinges/sliders and bounded cargo objects before conveyors, gear contact and long chains.

Impulse joints suit small editable mechanisms but can drift if the solver does not converge. Rapier's reduced-coordinate multibody approach can improve articulated-tree stability, with different topology/editing tradeoffs. Revisit it if larger arms fail stability tests; confirm the needed JavaScript motor interfaces first. An engine migration is justified only by a specific reproduced missing capability or benchmark failure after tuning, not by theoretical fidelity. [Rapier joint-constraint tradeoffs](https://rapier.rs/docs/user_guides/javascript/joint_constraints/)

## Sensors and honest educational behavior

Mount sensors in body-local frames and transform them from the current **physics** pose, not a rendered/interpolated pose or original build position. Capture one consistent sensor frame for the program tick.

For distance, cast a normalized ray to a configured maximum range, excluding the entire owning assembly and nonphysical UI/trigger geometry. Return distance plus validity, with an explicit no-hit state; no-hit must not become zero. Rapier supports query flags, groups, body exclusions and predicates. A multi-body robot needs assembly-wide filtering, not just one excluded chassis body. A later cone sensor can use a small fixed ray bundle, labeled as an approximation. [Rapier scene queries](https://rapier.rs/docs/user_guides/javascript/scene_queries/)

For line/color sensing, first find the surface below the sensor, then sample its authored line/color field at the hit's local coordinate. Use semantic surface data or a CPU-side color map prepared once; avoid GPU pixel readbacks in the control loop. Constrain height, direction and footprint, distinguish no surface from no line, and use a small sampling footprint if the lesson needs partial line coverage. A robot crossing a painted line must produce the same reading regardless of camera, shadow or display quality. Arbitrary user colors require an explicit matching/reflection rule instead of nearest display hue by accident.

Start ideal, with optional documented seeded noise/delay in a later lesson. Do not add unexplained randomness to imitate realism. Separate wheel encoder rotation from ground-truth travel once slip exists. In the ideal model they agree by assumption; in the dynamic model that difference can teach feedback. Validate sensor behavior at every supported maximum speed: at speed `v` and sample interval `T`, the rover moves `v*T` between samples, so a narrow stripe can be missed. Increase sampling or constrain lesson geometry deliberately.

GEARS is useful primary prior art: its own wiki explains that tire slippage makes straight travel imperfect and changes the learning level. The lesson here is to make that choice deliberate. Brickgineers should offer approachable ideal behavior first if its audience requires it, and introduce physical uncertainty when students can observe and respond to it. No GEARS code was copied. [GEARS official wiki](https://github.com/QuirkyCort/gears/wiki)

## Imagination pieces: turbo, thrusters and levitation

These are feasible within the existing engine, provided they have authored limits. They do not require aerodynamics or a full flight simulator. Every module should have a local attachment frame, command input, available/active/cooldown state and a short student-facing rule. Give direct buttons and programs the same underlying commands: for example, `boost`, `setThrust(percent)` and `setHoverHeight(studs)`. Module color/effects should reflect actual state rather than merely animate when clicked.

| Piece | Smallest useful implementation | What students can learn | Explicit boundary |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Turbo | Temporarily increases the drive model's bounded target speed or available force, then cools down. | Sequences, timed actions, speed/distance, stopping earlier at higher speed. | In ideal drive this is a speed rule, not engine power. It must still respect collision sweeps and the validated maximum speed. |
| Directional thruster | Apply bounded force along the module's transformed local axis on a dynamic body. Begin with one or two modules. | Direction, combinations of inputs, symmetry and controlled action/reaction effects. | Decide whether thrust acts at the center of mass for assisted control or at the mount for torque; those produce different behavior and cannot be interchanged silently. |
| Hover pad | Bounded height controller above a sensed support surface, with optional leveling assistance and lateral commands. | Height sensing, target/error, feedback, supported payload, energy/cooldown. | This is a fictional assistance mechanism; it does not demonstrate magnetic levitation or real hovercraft aerodynamics. |

Rapier provides forces, torques and impulses on dynamic bodies, including force at a point. Added forces persist across steps. A central actuator pass should clear/recompute its controlled forces and torques once per tick, then add all modules' contributions; one thruster must not erase another's force. An impulse is a one-time velocity change, whereas a sustained thruster supplies force. The installed `RigidBody` declarations confirm these APIs. [Rapier forces and impulses](https://rapier.rs/docs/user_guides/javascript/rigid_body_forces_and_impulses/)

A starter hover controller can use `Fup = clamp(m*g + kp*(targetHeight-measuredHeight) - kd*verticalSpeed, 0, maxLift)`. This is a proposed controller to tune, not a proven parameter set. A downward ray/short ray bundle supplies height; a maximum sensing range and defined support policy prevent the pad from acquiring distant ground through walls. If no ground is found, choose an explicit fallback such as controlled descent, not an infinite hover-height reading. Ceiling contact must stop ascent through collision and limited thrust, not pose teleportation. A level-assist torque can keep the rover approachable; label it an assistance feature because it changes what thrust placement teaches.

For a first hover lesson, cap target altitude, horizontal speed, acceleration, total lift and active modules; add a visible energy meter or cooldown counted in simulation ticks. Define what happens when energy runs out. Energy can be an intentionally fictional gameplay resource, with reproducible consumption rules and no claim about joules/battery chemistry. Adding ordinary bricks can increase required lift if the mass model is enabled, but should never unexpectedly change a previously ideal mode without explanation.

The first flying creation should therefore be a level-assisted hovering robot with move/turn/up/down commands, a finite operating volume and a follow camera. Unrestricted six-degree-of-freedom thrust, flips and orbital movement add control allocation, camera disorientation, high angular speeds, sensor range and recovery work; defer them until free flight is itself the lesson. A kinematic ideal-drive body will ignore thrust forces, so entering a dynamic hover/thruster simulation is a compiled-model choice made at restart, not a hidden runtime toggle.

Use a camera with a stable horizon, controlled follow distance, obstruction handling and an always-available focus/return control. Add an explicit recovery action that restores the last safe grounded spawn or authored spawn, clears motion and preserves the build. Test missed key releases, held boost, roof impacts, ground disappearance, leaving world bounds, falling, battery exhaustion and repeat resets. Put particles/glow behind the low graphics profile; flight needs only a small number of additional queries and one existing body unless the student builds articulated parts. Benchmark at maximum allowed speed/altitude and camera visibility, because a higher view can expose much more of a large world even when physics-body count is unchanged.

## Fixed-step scheduling and overload behavior

Use one simulation clock, initially 60 Hz. Order each tick consistently: read input/previous sensor snapshot, run a bounded program slice, apply motor commands, advance physics, sample sensors/events, then publish a throttled UI snapshot. New samples feed the next program tick. Rendering interpolates between simulation states and may run at 30 Hz without changing robot speed. Never advance the robot or interpreter according to rendered-frame count. R3R supports fixed timesteps, before/after-step hooks, manual stepping and interpolation. Its documentation warns that variable timestep can introduce instability. [R3R timestep and manual stepping](https://github.com/pmndrs/react-three-rapier#configuring-time-step-size)

**Installed-code detail:** `react-three-rapier.esm.js:883–916` clamps a frame delta to 0.5 seconds, then consumes the accumulated fixed steps in a loop. At 60 Hz that can entail about 30 physics steps after a stall. The existing character helper limits its own updates to three, but that is not a cap on the wrapper's world steps. Use a tested app-owned accumulator/manual stepping adapter or another verified bound; do not invent a `maxSubSteps` prop. Ensure automatic stepping is disabled when taking manual ownership.

Proposed initial overload policy: at most three physics ticks per rendered frame; if sustained load prevents keeping up, pause with a recoverable message rather than silently slowing timed programs or dropping essential control time. Record overload counts. Never increase `dt` dynamically to disguise lag. Test a fixed 30 Hz alternative only as a separate versioned simulation profile if 60 Hz fails the actual hardware gate; the contact and sensor behavior must be revalidated.

On hidden-tab/device-sleep transitions, stop input, pause simulation and reset wall-clock accumulation. Resume through a visible paused state. Browsers commonly stop animation callbacks and throttle timers in hidden tabs; an independent animation loop is still not a background execution guarantee. [MDN visibility behavior](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/API/Page_Visibility_API)

CCD helps fast dynamic bodies avoid tunneling and includes angular motion, at additional cost. Enable it selectively for the moving chassis or fast links/cargo after speed-vs-obstacle tests; it is not a fix for invalid spawn penetration, incorrect kinematic rotation, bad joints, or huge timesteps. Increasing CCD substeps adds more work. [Rapier CCD](https://rapier.rs/docs/user_guides/javascript/rigid_body_ccd/)

## Chromebook budgets and proof plan

Thirty students running one local rover each mostly means thirty independent client simulations plus shared loading/saving/network demand. Thirty robots colliding in one shared world is a different architecture and capacity test. Do not run every student's local physics everywhere or promise deterministic multiplayer from matching timesteps. First certify individual simulations, then a real 30-device class rehearsal. Shared physical interaction requires a separately specified authority model.

Keep existing static world parts instanced/batched where possible; transform the moving assembly as a group or update only its instance range. Instancing reduces rendering work but does not remove Rapier bodies or constraints. Keep per-tick state outside React reconciliation; send inspector/telemetry updates at perhaps 10 Hz. Reuse scratch vectors, buffers and collision shapes. R3F recommends instancing, reuse and demand rendering when idle; its example draw-call guidance is not a Chromebook certification threshold. [R3F performance guidance](https://github.com/pmndrs/react-three-fiber/blob/master/docs/advanced/scaling-performance.mdx)

Prefer a low graphics profile with DPR around 1, restrained shadows, limited transparency and no expensive postprocessing; preserve sensor semantics when degrading graphics. Pause or render on demand in idle Build mode. Batch rendering and reduce the backing-buffer size when fill rate is the bottleneck; avoid synchronous GPU reads in the steady-state loop. [MDN WebGL performance practices](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/API/WebGL_API/WebGL_best_practices)

Proposed targets to validate on the slowest supported school device:

| Measure | Initial target, not existing result |
| --- | --- |
| Visible frame cadence during normal driving/building | At least 30 fps; p95 frame interval at or below approximately 33.3 ms. Record p99 and worst stalls separately. |
| Physics work | p95 at or below 4 ms per 60 Hz tick for the release fixture. This leaves room for two ticks per 30 Hz frame; it is an allocation to test, not a prediction. |
| Program plus sensor work | p95 at or below 1 ms/tick, with hard instruction/query/event budgets. |
| Stop/manual-control responsiveness | p95 below 100 ms from input to visible response on the measured device. |
| Start/reset/recompile | p95 below 1 second for the supported robot envelope after assets load; responsive progress UI if work exceeds a frame. |
| Run lifecycle | No monotonic growth in body/joint/controller counts or retained geometry across 100 reset/edit/run cycles; investigate memory plateaus rather than requiring immediate WASM heap shrink. |
| Reliability | No simulation explosion, stale control after Stop, lost build, unbounded catch-up, unhandled exception, or unrecovered WebGL-context loss in the lesson rehearsal. |

An initial **test envelope** could be one rover with up to 100 ordinary bricks, two powered wheels, four sensors, and a static world up to the existing 1,000-brick document limit. Compile to one chassis for ideal/raycast drive; a hinge-wheel experiment adds wheel bodies and only the needed support bodies. This is a proposed first product envelope, not a new document limit or measured capacity. Also test 25/100/250-brick robots to discover the real boundary. Dynamic collider count, active contacts, joint count and visible geometry must be logged separately from brick count.

Benchmark matrix:

1. **Baseline control:** existing dense/mixed 250/500/1,000 worlds in Build and Explore, all intended environments, then robotics UI present but paused. Use identical camera paths and record exact SHA/configuration.
2. **Motion comparison:** same 25/100-brick rover and course with ideal drive, raycast chassis and hinge wheels. Include straight runs, in-place turns, reverse, asymmetric drive and repeated stop/start. Measure path error and contact behavior as well as frame time.
3. **Worst geometry:** long overhang, narrow passage, thin wall, inside corner, wheel touching chassis, steep ramp, neighboring slope seams, step heights expressed as fractions of wheel radius, ramp crest, edge fall and invalid spawn. Include maximum-size custom parts separately from high part counts.
4. **Sensors:** zero/max range, self-occlusion, blocked sensor, elevated/backward sensor, narrow stripes at maximum speed, tilted floor, multiple surfaces, no hit, and line visuals under each graphics profile.
5. **Lifecycle:** 100 resets, repeated drag/resize/recompile, remove a running motor, route leave/reentry, hidden tab, sleep/wake, touch/key release outside canvas, Stop during a busy program, and context loss/recovery.
6. **Mechanism growth:** only after the rover passes, test a hinged bridge, one-link arm, two-finger gripper, then 5/10/20 moving rigid groups and 5/10/20 joints with bounded cargo. These are diagnostic axes, not launch promises.
7. **Classroom:** use the real lowest-tier and typical managed Chromebooks with actual extensions, school Wi-Fi, 1366×768-class displays, battery/plugged states and thermal warmup. Run a full 30–45-minute lesson on 30 devices, including simultaneous cold loads, Run/Reset and save/reopen. Capture per-device failures and network evidence independently from local simulation timing.

Collect frame intervals, physics/interpreter/sensor durations, active/total bodies and colliders, contact/joint counts, queries per tick, draw calls/triangles, allocations/GC, memory trend, context-loss events, input latency and compile/reset duration. Use counters and bounded ring buffers; do not synchronously serialize the entire world each frame. Start with short diagnostic traces and longer unattended runs; tracing overhead itself must be noted. Chrome's CPU throttle is relative to the host and cannot stand in for actual target architecture; GPU, memory, browser policy and classroom load remain unproven on a desktop. [Chrome performance reference](https://developer.chrome.com/docs/devtools/performance/reference)

Move physics to a worker only if traces show physics/program work blocking the UI after reducing bodies/contacts and bounding work. A worker does not lower total computation or fix GPU load; it adds command timestamps, snapshot transfer, interpolation, teardown and pause synchronization. R3R's `updateLoop="independent"` is a separate animation loop, not a Web Worker. OffscreenCanvas, WebGPU, another renderer or another engine are not prerequisites for the first robotics slice.

## Explicit upgrade gates

- **Ideal → dynamic chassis:** students must climb ramps, fall, tip, carry weight or push objects; or the visible conservative envelope makes meaningful student builds unusable.
- **Ground rover → assisted hover/thrust:** imaginative modules are ready for the next slice; first certify bounded force, ground/ceiling sensing, energy/cooldown, camera and recovery. Unrestricted flight remains a separate upgrade.
- **Raycast → physical wheel hinges:** lessons depend on wheel-radius obstacle climbing, true side contact, unsupported-wheel behavior, axle reaction or wheel/mechanism contact that rays cannot represent convincingly.
- **Uncalibrated motors → bounded torque model:** lessons ask why a motor stalls, why gearing changes lifting ability, or how load changes acceleration. Require quantitative fixtures and verified API/control behavior first.
- **Small impulse mechanisms → multibody evaluation:** arm drift or stability remains unacceptable within validated timestep/mass/solver budgets; confirm binding capability and graph restrictions before switching.
- **Main thread → worker:** measured physics/program CPU time causes interaction failures after simpler optimizations. Preserve identical command/sensor contracts.
- **Increase launch capacity:** only after the exact larger fixture passes on the actual lowest-tier device and the full class rehearsal. A desktop demo with many cubes is not sufficient evidence.

The key expandable seam is the compiled assembly plus motor/sensor contract. That allows an approachable first rover to grow into genuine mechanisms without tying the editable brick document to one physics controller or damaging a student's work on reset.
